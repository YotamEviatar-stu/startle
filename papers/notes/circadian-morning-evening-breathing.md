# Morning vs evening awake resting breathing: is there a literature prior?

*Digest compiled 2026-09-27. Sources: Europe PMC / PMC full text and publisher abstracts. Each row says whether it was read as **FT** (full text) or **Abs** (abstract only).*

## Verdict

**Weak, and only indirect.** The one study that measured awake breathing frequency at the same morning and evening clock times as ours found **slower breathing in the morning**: 14.9 vs 16.4 breaths/min, about a 10 % longer cycle (4.0 s vs 3.7 s), P ≤ 0.0005 (Gerst 2011). But it studied people with obstructive sleep apnoea, breathing through a mask at +3 mmHg CO2, not healthy people breathing room air through a cannula. The controlled constant-routine studies find **no endogenous circadian rhythm in breathing frequency, VT or VE** (Spengler 2000, Stephenson 2000). So if the morning prior holds, it comes from sleep/wake history, arousal and "masking" factors, not from the circadian clock. Nothing found addresses breath-to-breath **variability** by time of day. Direction if any: morning slower, never faster.

Strand notes:
- **(b) Mateika-lab claim.** The sentence "a circadian oscillation in minute ventilation and its components … nadir in the early morning" comes from El-Chami 2014 ([doi:10.1152/japplphysiol.00681.2014](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00681.2014); full text 403'd). Its reference list ([Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25213638)) points to Mortola 2004, Spengler 2000, Stephenson 2000, Vargas 2001, Raschke 1989 and Gerst 2011.
  - Mortola 2004 ([doi:10.1007/s00421-003-0978-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00421-003-0978-0), Abs only) is a review. Its human breathing-pattern sources are the constant-routine studies above plus Bosco 2003 ([doi:10.1089/152702903769192269](https://doi.org/10.1089/152702903769192269): VE rhythmic in normoxia, n = 6, f not in the abstract).
  - Once traced back to primaries, the "early-morning nadir" rests on **VE/VT**, not on frequency. The only frequency number is Gerst 2011.
- **(c) Post-awakening.** No study found measuring awake breathing *pattern* minutes to hours after morning waking in healthy people.
  - Fuse 1999 ([doi:10.1046/j.1440-1843.1999.00163.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1440-1843.1999.00163.x), Abs) found lower resting VE after sleep than before sleep in **OSA** patients (n = 19). In 12 controls it reported only "no circadian variation" in chemoresponses.

## Caveats and confounds

- **Session order / novelty.** Anxiety raises respiratory rate preferentially over VT, and more so in high-trait-anxiety subjects (Masaoka & Homma 1997, [doi:10.1016/S0167-8760(97)00052-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0167-8760(97)00052-4), Abs). A first session, if evening, would bias toward "faster evening" with no clock involved.
  - Spengler 2000 used two acclimation days precisely to familiarise subjects.
  - Gerst 2011 is the only morning-vs-evening study with a PM session *after* the AM sessions, and the difference persisted there.
- **Instrumentation.** A mouthpiece + nose-clip raises VT, TI and TE and **lowers f** relative to unobtrusive RIP (Perez & Tobin 1985, [doi:10.1152/jappl.1985.59.5.1515](https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1985.59.5.1515), Abs). Every study above used a mouthpiece or mask, so absolute rates do not transfer to a nasal cannula. The within-subject direction may still transfer.
- **Arousal, drowsiness, meals, posture, CO2.**
  - Residual drowsiness alters breathing. Spengler EEG-screened for it; ordinary-day studies did not.
  - Gerst fasted subjects 4–6 h and held them supine, and its frequency data are under mild hypercapnia.
  - Our sessions differ in time since the last meal and time since waking. Each is an uncontrolled masking factor, and the literature treats these, rather than the clock, as the likely source of any morning/evening difference.
- **Population.** The only positive frequency result comes from OSA patients, whose overnight intermittent hypoxia itself changes next-morning ventilatory control (Gerst 2011; Fuse 1999). Generalising to healthy subjects is untested.

## Pipeline consequence

None. This is a literature prior about a **condition difference**, not a processing choice. It changes nothing in `extras/emg_raw_potentiation.py` or `extras/trial_epochs.py`, and it gives no licence to make any gate or threshold condition-aware. It bears only on how an eventual evening-vs-morning breath-duration difference would be *interpreted*: plausible, with a weak prior of about 10 % slower in the morning, and confoundable with session order.
